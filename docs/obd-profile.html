Smooth AF · what the car can tell us

The Driving Fingerprint

A score says how smooth. A fingerprint says who — the shape of your inputs, read a few times a second. The richest signal isn't any single number; it's the accelerator pedal traced through a corner, against the g-force the car is actually pulling. That one pairing separates a clean line from a clumsy one — so it's where this starts.

The thesis

Reading a corner

Same bend, same speed limit, two drivers. The score engine today sees roughly equal peak g. The pedal trace is where they stop being equal — and a dongle plus the phone's motion sensors can watch it the whole way through.

● Brake & gun

In too hot, then a stab

Full lift off the gas, brake hard into the apex, then slam the throttle to haul it back out. The g-arc spikes late and lopsided; the car is never settled.

● Carry the line

Steady in, smooth out

Ease off the gas, trail a little speed to the apex on the racing line, then roll the throttle back on progressively. The g-arc is one clean, symmetric hump — the car stays planted.

Accelerator pedal — OBD 0x49 Lateral g — phone motion Speed — OBD 0x0D

The tell is pedal jerk — how violently the foot moves — read against where you are in the g-arc. A near-vertical pedal edge at corner exit is the "gun"; a smooth ramp on the same part of the arc is the racing line. Two drivers can post the same average and the same top speed; only the trace tells them apart.

The raw material

Every channel we can read

A standard ELM327 dongle speaks OBD-II Mode 01 — a fixed menu of PIDs. Not everything you'd want is on that menu (brakes and steering aren't), so the phone's own sensors carry the rest. Three tiers, honestly labelled.

The trick

Filling the blind spots

The two channels you'd most want for corner technique — brake and steering — are exactly the two a generic dongle can't read. It doesn't matter: the pedal (OBD) and the phone's motion sensors reconstruct both, and arguably describe the driving better than a raw sensor angle would.

Braking, without a brake sensor

✕ no brake-pedal PID

You don't need the pedal angle to know a brake was hard or smooth. When the accelerator reads zero, the speed is dropping, and the phone feels a backward g, that is a braking event — and the shape of that g curve is exactly the "early and gentle" vs "late and hard" distinction.

pedal = 0+speed ↓+−g→braking, graded

The corner, without a steering sensor

✕ no steering-angle PID

Lateral g is the turn as the car actually felt it, and yaw is how fast it rotated. Lay the pedal trace over that arc and you have the whole technique: what the gas did through entry, apex and exit, and whether the car stayed settled or got upset.

lateral g+yaw+speed+pedal→corner technique

The catch, and the plan

What it takes to get there

One real constraint: the dongle is slow

An ELM327 is a serial line pretending to be Bluetooth — one request, one reply, roughly 4–10 readings a second total, shared across every PID you ask for. Poll ten channels and each one crawls. So the design is two-speed: a small fast set for technique — pedal, speed, rpm, plus the phone's 60 Hz motion — polled as hard as the adapter allows, and a slow set for context — temps, fuel, gear — sampled every few seconds where lag doesn't matter. The corner read lives entirely in the fast set.

1
Record the pedal. Promote 0x49 to a first-class fast channel and stamp it onto every sample, the way rpm and throttle already are. Nothing is visible yet — but from here on, every drive is banking the trace.
2
Segment corners, read the trace. Cut the drive into corner windows from the lateral-g arc, then score each on pedal jerk, roll-on smoothness and carried speed. This is the racing-line read shipping for real.
3
Add the context dimensions. Fold in the slow set — fuel, temps, gear — for mechanical sympathy and efficiency, rounding the score out from "how harsh" to a full fingerprint.
